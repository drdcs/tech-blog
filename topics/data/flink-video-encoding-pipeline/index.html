Encoding at Scale: A Flink Pipeline for Video & Image Processing

One high-quality master goes in. Thousands of renditions come out — every resolution, bitrate, codec, language, and device your catalog needs. Here's how I'd wire that explosion as a stateful Flink fan-out pipeline, split by segment, quality, and a lot more.

💡 1. The thesis

When you press play on a large streaming service, the file you receive was never encoded once. Somewhere upstream, a single pristine mezzanine master was shredded into small pieces, each piece encoded many times over — different resolutions, bitrates, codecs, audio tracks — then reassembled, packaged, encrypted, and pushed to a CDN close to you.

That "encode a title" job is not one job. It's a fan-out: one input event expands into thousands of independent encode tasks, all of which must finish, pass quality control, and be stitched back together before a title can ship. That shape — fan out, track completion, reassemble — is exactly what a stateful stream processor is good at. This post wires it up as an Apache Flink pipeline.

Continues from Thinking in Flink. That piece was Kafka → stateful logic → Bigtable. This one takes the same keyed state, backpressure, and exactly-once machinery and points it at an encoding farm. Same brain, louder workload.

📈 2. One master, thousands of outputs

Say a two-hour title arrives as a single mezzanine file. To serve every viewer well, you need a matrix of outputs, not a single MP4:

  • A bitrate ladder — maybe 8–12 rungs from a low-bandwidth mobile stream up to 4K HDR.
  • Multiple codecs — AVC for reach, HEVC and AV1 for efficiency on capable devices.
  • Several audio tracks and dozens of subtitle/caption languages.
  • SDR and HDR variants, different frame rates, and device-specific packaging.

Multiply that out and a single title becomes thousands of encoded artifacts. Encoding the whole two hours serially, per rung, would take far too long, so you chunk the master into short segments and encode them in parallel across a large farm — then the hard part becomes coordination, not compression.

The real problem is bookkeeping. Compression is a solved, embarrassingly-parallel task. Knowing which of the 12,000 chunk-renditions for a title are done, which failed, and when the whole title is ready to package is the distributed-systems problem. That's the part Flink owns.

🏗️ 3. The pipeline, end to end

Let me walk you through the whole journey before we zoom into any one piece. Picture a single master file landing at one end. We ingest it, split it into short segments, then fan each segment out into every rendition we need. Each of those gets encoded, quality-checked, and stitched back together per rendition. Then we package it into streaming formats, encrypt it, and push it out to the CDN. One file goes in; thousands of ready-to-stream artifacts come out.

Fig 01 — The dual pipeline: a video path and an image path, both Kafka-driven and orchestrated by a stateful Flink job

So here's how I'd read this with you, top to bottom. That dashed line across the top is the control plane — Kafka to Flink to the metadata store to monitoring. Notice it never touches a single media byte; all it shuffles around is events. Below it are the two solid lanes, and the important bit is that they're completely independent: the video path (A) and the image path (B) scale on their own and fail on their own, and the only thing they share is the event bus. That's on purpose — a 4K encode getting stuck should never hold up a thumbnail refresh.

And where does Flink actually sit in all this? Right across the middle. Every stage you see is a Flink operator (or a quick call out to the encode farm), and the arrows between them aren't carrying video — they're carrying tiny status notes like “segment 41 of title T is ready” or “the 720p/AV1 version of segment 41 just passed QC.” The heavy media never rides through Flink at all; it lives in object storage. Flink only moves the metadata and the state.

✂️ 4. How we split the work

Now the fun question — the one I'd actually whiteboard with you: when we “fan out,” what are we fanning out along? Segment and quality are the two everyone names first. But a real encoding pipeline splits along a lot more than that, and each of these axes ends up baked into the key that names one single unit of work. Here's the full list I keep in my head:

Split axis What it means Why it exists
Segment Short GOP- or shot-aligned chunks of the timeline Parallelism — encode a 2-hour title in the time of one chunk
Quality (bitrate) Each rung of the bitrate ladder Adaptive streaming picks a rung to match bandwidth
Resolution 235p → 4K spatial sizes Serve tiny screens and living-room 4K from one catalog
Codec / profile AVC, HEVC, VP9, AV1 and their profiles Reach on old devices, efficiency on new ones
Scene complexity Per-shot / per-title bitrate assigned by content Spend bits where the picture is hard, save them where it's flat
Dynamic range SDR vs HDR (HDR10, Dolby Vision) & color space Correct tone/color per display capability
Frame rate 24 / 30 / 50 / 60 fps variants Source cadence and high-motion / sports content
Audio track Stereo, 5.1, Atmos; multiple languages Localization and premium sound
Subtitle / caption Dozens of languages, forced narratives, SDH Accessibility and global catalogs
Packaging / device CMAF, HLS, DASH per device family Different players want different manifests/containers
DRM Widevine / FairPlay / PlayReady Studio content protection per platform
Region / CDN Which edges get which renditions first Latency and licensing windows by territory

If I had to pick a favorite, it's scene complexity. Instead of slapping the same bitrate ladder on every title, you look at the actual content — sometimes shot by shot — and spend bits based on how hard each scene is to compress. A quiet talking-head interview and a fireworks finale should never get the same treatment, and that's exactly why you hear “per-title” and “per-shot” optimization come up so often. The nice part: that analysis is just one more Flink operator up front, feeding its numbers into the fan-out.

Fig 02 — The full platform blueprint: every video/image stage plus the Flink topology variants (per-shot, per-title, QC loop, packaging)

This blueprint is where those axes stop being a table and turn into real jobs. And notice it isn't one giant DAG doing everything — it's a whole family of topologies (per-shot adaptive, per-title orchestration, a QC-and-reprocessing loop, packaging) that all chat over Kafka. Depending on how expensive an axis is, it might just be a partition key, a value we broadcast as config, or an entire sub-pipeline of its own.

⚡ 5. Why Flink runs the fan-out

You could orchestrate this with a workflow engine and a queue, and plenty of teams do. But the encoding problem has streaming-shaped requirements that map cleanly onto the same Flink concepts from the previous essay:

  • Keyed state — key by (title, segment, rendition); each key tracks its own encode lifecycle and retries.
  • Completion joins — Flink holds the running tally of "how many chunk-renditions of title T are done" and fires only when the set is complete.
  • Watermarks as progress — a watermark meaning "the master is fully chunked" lets the assembler know no more segments are coming, so a rendition can finalize.
  • Backpressure — when the encode farm saturates, backpressure propagates up the topology and you can see exactly where the queue is building.
  • Exactly-once assembly — encode results may be replayed after failure, so the assembler and packager must be idempotent, keyed on deterministic chunk IDs.

Fig 03 — The video path as a Flink topology: operator DAG, worker/slot runtime view, state & checkpointing, and the reprocessing loop

This is the same five bullets above, drawn as a real job. Two things worth calling out: the parallelism is uneven on purpose — the encode operator runs far wider than packaging or DRM because that's where the work is — and the side output → dead-letter → reprocess loop is a first-class part of the graph, not an afterthought. Failures are just another stream.

🔑 6. The keyed-state model

The heart of the pipeline is a single idea: every unit of work has a deterministic identity, and Flink keeps state per identity. A natural composite key is: 

key = (title_id, segment_id, rendition)
rendition = (resolution, bitrate_rung, codec, dynamic_range, frame_rate)

Keyed by that, each Flink subtask owns a slice of the work and answers questions locally: is this chunk-rendition encoded? did it pass QC? how many retries has it burned? A second keying — by (title_id, rendition) — maintains a completion counter so the assembler fires exactly once when every segment of a rendition is present. Deterministic keys are what make replays safe: re-processing the same chunk overwrites the same state and republishes the same artifact, so at-least-once delivery behaves like exactly-once output.

🧩 7. Operator DAG & stitch code

The last image is the useful one for engineers: it zooms all the way in — the operator DAG, the job graph with per-operator parallelism, the cluster layout (JobManager, TaskManagers, RocksDB-on-S3 state backend), the reprocessing DAG, and a compact DataStream API stitch that wires the whole job together.

Fig 04 — Operator DAG, job-graph parallelism, cluster layout, and the DataStream stitch code

Below is that stitch code, cleaned up. It's the whole job in one read: source, enrich, broadcast config, fan out, encode via Async I/O, QC, join, package, encrypt, sink — with exactly-once checkpointing to RocksDB on S3. 

StreamExecutionEnvironment env = StreamExecutionEnvironment.getExecutionEnvironment();

// Exactly-once checkpointing to a durable state backend (RocksDB on S3)
env.enableCheckpointing(60_000, CheckpointingMode.EXACTLY_ONCE);
env.getCheckpointConfig().setMinPauseBetweenCheckpoints(30_000);
env.setStateBackend(new EmbeddedRocksDBStateBackend(true));
env.getCheckpointConfig().setCheckpointStorage("s3://flink-checkpoints/video");

// Encoding config (bitrate ladder, codecs, QC rules) broadcast to every task
BroadcastStream<EncodeConfig> config = env
    .fromSource(configSource, WatermarkStrategy.noWatermarks(), "encode-config")
    .broadcast(CONFIG_DESCRIPTOR);

DataStream<Packaged> published = env
    .fromSource(kafkaVideoEvents,
        WatermarkStrategy.<IngestEvent>forBoundedOutOfOrderness(Duration.ofSeconds(5))
            .withTimestampAssigner((e, ts) -> e.eventTimeMs()),
        "video.events")
    .keyBy(IngestEvent::titleId)
    .process(new PreProcessFunction())      // enrich + dedup per title
    .connect(config)
    .process(new FanOutFunction())          // one chunk -> N renditions
    .keyBy(ChunkKey::of)
    .process(new EncodingAsyncDispatch())   // Async I/O -> encode farm
    .keyBy(ChunkKey::of)
    .process(new QualityCheckFunction())    // VMAF / sync / tech rules
    .keyBy(RenditionKey::of)
    .process(new CompletionJoinFunction())  // join all chunks per rendition
    .process(new PackagingFunction())       // HLS / DASH / CMAF manifests
    .process(new DrmEncryptFunction());     // Widevine / PlayReady / FairPlay

published.sinkTo(s3Sink);                   // deterministic object paths
published.sinkTo(cdnPublishSink);           // emit publish.cdn event

env.execute("Flink Video Encoding Pipeline");

The completion join is where the bookkeeping lives — keyed by (title, rendition), it tallies encoded-and-passed segments and fires exactly once when the set is whole. Failed chunks leave through a side output to a dead-letter topic; a reprocess job re-encodes them and feeds results back in. 

public class CompletionJoinFunction
        extends KeyedProcessFunction<RenditionKey, EncodeResult, RenditionReady> {

    private ValueState<Integer> expectedSegments;   // set when the master is chunked
    private MapState<Long, Boolean> doneSegments;    // idempotent per-segment set

    @Override
    public void processElement(EncodeResult r, Context ctx, Collector<RenditionReady> out)
            throws Exception {

        if (!r.passedQc) {
            ctx.output(QC_FAILURES, r);          // side output -> dead-letter -> reprocess
            return;
        }

        doneSegments.put(r.segmentId, true);     // replay-safe: same id, same slot

        int expected = expectedSegments.value() == null ? -1 : expectedSegments.value();
        if (expected > 0 && size(doneSegments) == expected) {
            out.collect(new RenditionReady(r.titleId, r.rendition)); // fire once
        }
    }
}
Why the composite key matters. keyBy((title, rendition)) is what lets Flink shard the completion bookkeeping across the cluster and survive failures — the same trick as the hot-key discussion in Thinking in Flink, except here a "hot key" is a blockbuster title everyone re-encodes at once.

🖼️ 8. The image / artwork side

The same fan-out shape runs for stills. Every poster, thumbnail, and hero image is generated in many variants from one source asset — and, because artwork is personalized and A/B tested, the matrix is bigger than people expect.

  • Size — grid thumbnails, detail-page heroes, TV-lane tiles.
  • Aspect ratio & crop — 16:9, 2:3, square, with focal-point aware cropping so faces survive.
  • Format — AVIF and WebP for modern clients, JPEG as fallback.
  • Quality — perceptual quality target per size to control bytes on the wire.
  • Locale / overlay — localized title treatments, badges, maturity ratings baked in.
  • A/B variant — multiple artwork candidates per title for engagement testing.
Same shape, smaller payload. Path B in Fig 01 is exactly this fan-out — one source still explodes into the size × crop × format × locale × A/B matrix, keyed by (asset_id, variant). Because images are cheap, this path runs as a lower-latency lane of the same platform.

Images are lighter and faster than video, so this often runs as a lower-latency lane of the same platform — new asset event in, keyed by (asset_id, variant), fan out, generate, publish. Same pattern, smaller blast radius.

🩹 9. Production pain points

The scars here rhyme with the ones from Thinking in Flink, just wearing a media costume:

  • Straggler chunks. A single complex segment (dense motion, grain) can take far longer than its neighbors and hold up an entire rendition's assembly. Timers + speculative retries keep the tail bounded.
  • Encode-farm saturation. Launch a big catalog re-encode and the farm backs up; backpressure climbs the topology and checkpoints slow down. Admission control and per-title concurrency caps matter.
  • Poison media. A malformed source or an unsupported codec path fails deterministically — retrying forever wastes the farm. Route it to a dead-letter / manual-review side output fast.
  • Rendition explosion cost. Every new codec or ladder rung multiplies compute and storage across the whole catalog. Per-title/per-shot optimization exists partly to stop paying for bitrates nobody streams.
  • QC rejects → re-encode loops. A VMAF miss sends a chunk back through, and without idempotent keys those loops double-write. Deterministic chunk IDs keep retries safe.
  • Exactly-once at the sink. Just like Bigtable upserts, the packager/publisher must be idempotent — the pipeline is at-least-once, and deterministic artifact paths are what make the output look exactly-once.

✨ 10. Closing

Video and image delivery looks like a compression story, but at scale it's a coordination story: fan one master out along a dozen axes, track completion across thousands of tasks, reassemble deterministically, and publish exactly-once to the edge. That's a stateful streaming problem, and it's why the same Flink concepts — keyed state, watermarks, backpressure, idempotent sinks — keep showing up whether the payload is a Bigtable row or a 4K HDR chunk.

If you want the mental models behind those concepts, start with Thinking in Flink (and When Spark Is Enough). This pipeline is just that brain, turned up to catalog scale.
